Most of the time when we are learning something, we are learning it with the hope that it will be useful later. The problem is, we are not sure _how_ it will be useful or _when_ exactly we will need it. That uncertainty often makes it hard to concentrate on lectures or whatever we are trying to learn.


I looked this up online and apparently there’s actually a name for it in learning psychology — it’s called a **relevance problem**. Basically, the brain is wired to pay more attention to things that feel immediately useful (“just-in-time learning”), and it tends to push aside things that only seem like they might be useful someday (“just-in-case learning”). A few articles I read mentioned that when relevance is missing, motivation naturally drops ([Harvard Graduate School of Education](https://www.gse.harvard.edu/news/uk/16/06/making-learning-relevant)).


So I was just thinking — what if I try to imagine scenarios where this knowledge could actually be applied? Where it might be needed or where it could happen? Even though doing this is difficult, I’ve seen in some research videos that imagining is almost the same as doing it.


I also came across something interesting: studies using brain scans show that when you vividly imagine doing an action, many of the same areas in the brain activate as if you were actually doing it. This is called **mental simulation**, and it’s apparently why athletes and musicians practice visualization as part of their training ([Psychology Today](https://www.psychologytoday.com/us/blog/brain-wise/201201/mental-simulation) | [National Library of Medicine](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC2907136/)). The more concrete and detailed the imagined scenario, the more the brain treats it as “real.”


I’m not sure if this is going to be something I’ll actually manage to do with everything I’m trying to learn. But I’ll try. Even though I’m someone who usually lacks concentration in such cases, that’s exactly the reason I’m writing this down — to push myself toward trying.
