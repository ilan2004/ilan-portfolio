Most of the time when we are learning something, we are learning it with the hope that it will be useful later. The problem is, we are not sure _how_ it will be useful or _when_ exactly we will need it. That uncertainty often makes it hard to concentrate on lectures or whatever we are trying to learn.


I looked this up online and apparently there’s actually a name for it in learning psychology — it’s called a **relevance problem** or the contrast between “just-in-case” and “just-in-time” learning. Essentially, our brains tend to ignore information that isn’t clearly useful now. One post I read put it simply: “Just-in-case knowledge is at best inefficient, and at worst a huge waste of our collective time.” Meanwhile, “just-in-time learning” happens _when we actually need it_, and immediately applying it makes it stick better ([source](https://www.grahammann.net/blog/just-in-case-vs-just-in-time-learning)).


So I was just thinking — what if I try to imagine scenarios where this knowledge could actually be applied? Where it might be needed or where it could happen? Even though doing this is difficult, I’ve seen in some research videos that imagining is almost the same as doing it.


I also came across something interesting: brain imaging studies show that when you vividly imagine doing something, many of the same neural areas light up as if you were actually doing it. This concept—**mental simulation** or **motor imagery**—is not just theoretical. Olympians and trained experts use visualization as part of their routine. Imaging an action activates regions like the supplementary motor area, parietal cortex, and cerebellum—basically the same networks you'd use for physical movement ([source](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Motor_imagery)).


I’m not sure if this is going to be something I’ll actually manage to do with everything I’m trying to learn. But I’ll try. Even though I’m someone who usually lacks concentration in such cases, that’s exactly the reason I’m writing this down — to push myself toward trying.
